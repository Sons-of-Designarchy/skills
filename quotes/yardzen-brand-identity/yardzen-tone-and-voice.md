- Contractions always - Active voice - Numbers over adjectives - Exclamation points earned, roughly one per message and never in a sentence carrying information - "We" 
Our Brand Voice 
Expert and elevated. Has the confidence of someone who's seen tens of thousands of yards (as we have). Our brand voice, personified: Picture a landscape architect. She's well trained. A graduate of Harvard School of Landscape Architecture, she's worked on high-end residential projects and can talk specifics — like why the patio should sit eighteen inches lower or why the firepit doesn't adhere to setback rules. All without making you feel stupid for asking. She's warm but not chatty. She answers the question you asked, then the question you didn't know to ask, then stops. She's in tune with design trends. Ethos 
Aspiration but anchored on a specific. Or put another way: reaching language that's held up by details. 
Mid: An outdoor retreat that transforms how you live. 
Stronger: The kind of yard that pulls you outside on a Wednesday. All anchored around the Heritage Oak that was there 200 years before Amy and Dan moved into their 1920's Los Angeles Tudor.

Note: Any Instagram account can say gravel gardens are having a moment. Yardzen says why: water restrictions in CA, lower maintenance, etc. 
Rhythm 
The brand voice is allowed longer, more musical sentences than the human voice. Declaratives We say what's true. "Your yard should be the room you use most" is a brand sentence. "We think your yard could maybe be a great space" is not. What our voice doesn't do 
Hard sell. Beg. Manufacture urgency. Use exclamation points to generate energy. Disparage competition. Brag. 
Vocabulary The brand voice intentionally uses words that convey expertise. Canopy, threshold, massing, sited, provenance, grade, hardscape, pergola, shade structure. Landscape architecture has a beautiful vocabulary and we should use it! 
Aspirational words in the middle (
transform, oasis, sanctuary, dream, escape
) are available to the brand voice but use them once per piece and only with a concrete data point. Brand Voice: Writing to People Who Haven't Paid 
Optimistic, knowledgeable. Can be a little bold. 
Let's assume our reader has a yard they don't love and no idea what fixing that costs. They're deciding between us, a local designer, or more realistically, another year of doing nothing. Our job is to make the possibility clear and the path believable — in the same breath. 
The most persuasive thing we do is be experts on all things landscaping. A paragraph explaining why permeable pavers are preferred in southern California, or what heat islands are, or how a side yard can add functional square footage.

Brand Voice: Writing to People Who Have Paid 
The client already bought a design. Time to tone it down. Selling to them now suggests we're still selling when they just want us to create a great design. 
Pare back the language. At this point a word like oasis can feel like marketing or signal an automation. Notifications: They have one job. Instead of: Great news! Your design journey continues. Your renderings have arrived and we can't wait for you to explore your new outdoor oasis! 
Write: Your renderings are ready. 
Never let a system message impersonate a person. More on this below. It's the most damaging thing we can do to trust. The Human Voice 
Exceedingly helpful. Warm, direct, and always referencing this client's specific project. Informed. Specific. 
Optimistic, but anchored on the actual project. A Cost Advisor can be both excited about a plan and honest about what the slope is going to cost. We shouldn't see those as in tension. Being direct about the hard parts is what makes us feel supportive. 
Guiding principles for our human voice: 
1. Say it shorter Never make someone read three paragraphs to find out whether the news is good. 
2. Know things 
We're the expert in the room.

Instead of: We'll figure out the best spot for your patio. 
Write: We'll site the patio where it gets afternoon shade and drains away from the house. 
The occasional industry word signals our competence to our clients: tree canopy, grade, habitat-supporting plants, heritage tree, retaining wall, sited, spec. Use them. 
3. Be on their side 
Homeowners are spending real money. And they're anxious. Particularly with build, they're watching for the moment we start selling them. Don't let that happen. 4. Be warm, not cute 
We really like our clients. We don't perform liking them. No emojis. 
Note: Use exclamation points sparingly. Don't open and close with them. OK to match a client's tone if they use exclamation points. 5. Own it when it goes sideways 
At least one thing, however small, is likely to go wrong in every project. This is home renovation. Estimates come in high. Plant deliveries are delayed. Name the problem quickly, explain the cause plainly and then go straight to solutions/options. Don't apologize twice. Don't use passive voice to hide who did what. 
6. No message goes out without something specific to this client's project in it 
Not the project name or the phase. That's not specific enough. Something only a person looking at this yard would know. The oak. The slope. The dog. Instead of: Hi Sarah, just checking in to see if you had any questions about your design! 
Write: Hi Sarah, I wanted to flag one thing before you review. We moved the fire pit off the property line to keep it legal for your county, which pushed the seating area a few feet toward the house. Curious what you think of that trade. 
Note: There's always something specific to say. 
7. Contractions Always. Writing without them reads like a terms-of-service page.

The Details 
First person. "We" for what the company did. "Your designer" when referring to your colleague, who created the design. Open with the update. Instead of: Hi Mark, hope you're having a great week! I wanted to reach out with a quick update on where things stand with your project. 
Write: Hi Mark, your revision is ready and there's one thing I want to walk you through. 
Always say what happens next and when. Every message ends with a clear next step owned by someone specific, with a date. Good: "I'll have this back to you Thursday" 
Bad: "We'll be in touch soon." Never be combative. When a client reacts to a design, turn the reaction into design language that assumes their feedback is part of the process. Good: "I've noted that you want it to feel less formal" Bad: "I see you didn't like it" Own errors. Don't point fingers. 
Good:
 
"We misread the setback on your lot. Here's the corrected plan and what it changes." 
Bad: "An error was identified." 
Worst: "Your designer misread the setback on your lot." 
Be specific. 
Good:
 
"Forty linear feet of retaining wall" Less Good: "significant grading work." Human Voice: Writing to People Who Haven't Paid

Be specific about any information we've gathered, whether in a Design Profile Quiz or on a consult call. Don't send a message that doesn't reflect specific project/client-shared information. Human Voice: Writing to People Who Have Paid 
The client is now thinking about spending real money on their build. They're not skeptical anymore. They're anxious, and we're now earning the right to participate in their build. What they want to know, always in this order: What happened. What it means for my project. What's next and when. 
Delivering work. 
Hi Priya, Revision 1 is ready. Your designer kept the oak and built the patio around it, which cost about three feet of lawn but keeps the shade you said you wanted for summer. Take a look and tell me what's working and what isn't. I'll hold Thursday to make changes. 
Delivering a hard number. 
Hi Dan, the estimate came in at $84,000, which is above the range we discussed. The driver of the higher number is the grading. Your slope needs about forty more linear feet of retaining wall than we assumed. There are three ways to bring it down. I can jump on a call in 15 minutes to talk through them. 
Delivering a delay.. 
Hi Alexis, your design is running two days behind as we found an inaccuracy in your house model. That doesn't change your build timeline. Sorry for the shuffle. I'll check in on design progress in the morning and follow up with a confirmation about the new delivery estimate. 
Automations: Should be actively avoided if it's signed by a person. If nobody has time to manually write an email, send it from Yardzen instead.
